Policy Brief · Econ 306 · May 2020

Using Geothermal Energy to Cut Carbon Emissions and Protect the World's Coral Reefs

The problem
Carbon emissions from burning fossil fuels are making the ocean more acidic, and acidic water stops coral reefs from building their skeletons. The ocean absorbs about 30% of the CO₂ we release; that CO₂ lowers seawater pH and removes the carbonate corals need to grow.
Why it matters
Reefs shelter 25%+ of marine life and protect coastlines from storm waves, a service worth an estimated $9 billion a year. Once lost, they are expensive to replace: replanting the Great Barrier Reef alone would cost about $200 billion.
The question
Which clean energy source cuts these emissions most efficiently, and what policy would get firms to switch to it?
The answer
Geothermal energy: it runs 24/7, costs less to build than coal, and emits a fraction of coal's carbon. The brief recommends a tax on firms that haven't adopted it by 2030, plus subsidies for geothermal heat pumps where plants can't be built.

Cost to Build, per Kilowatt

Overnight capital cost, 2019 $/kW · U.S. EIA, Feb 2020 [6]
Geothermal costs ~31% less to build than coal per kW ($2,521 vs $3,676). Wind and solar now cost less to build, but only generate when the wind blows or the sun shines; geothermal runs around the clock.

Life-Cycle Carbon Emissions

Pounds of CO₂-equivalent per kWh generated · Union of Concerned Scientists [5]
Geothermal emits ≈0.1 lb CO₂e/kWh, about 14–36× less than coal. Its sulfur dioxide (SO₂) emissions are also ~30× lower per MWh than coal, the largest U.S. source.

Cost per Unit of Electricity (LCOE)

California, plants entering service in 2009 · nominal $/MWh, utility-owned · California Energy Commission [7]
In the CEC's 2009 comparison, both geothermal technologies cost less per MWh than small hydro and both solar options, second only to onshore wind. Solar costs have fallen sharply since: EIA's 2020 build cost for solar PV is $1,313/kW [6].

Policy Recommendation

What the brief proposes
  1. 2030 adoption deadlineFirms that haven't adopted geothermal technology by 2030 pay a tax, putting a price on continued fossil-fuel emissions.
  2. Subsidy where plants can't be builtGeothermal plants must sit near usable underground heat, so firms elsewhere get a subsidy for geothermal heat pumps, which use 25–50% less electricity than conventional heating and cooling [8].
  3. Why geothermalSteady 24/7 output at predictable prices, near-zero emissions, and equipment lasting 20+ years [8].
Economic logic: a Pigouvian tax makes polluters pay for the external cost of emissions, while the subsidy lowers the cost of switching for firms with no other clean option.

Trade-offs

Weighed in the brief's analysis

Advantages

  • Generates power 24 hours a day, unlike wind and solar.
  • Life-cycle emissions of ≈0.1 lb CO₂e/kWh versus 1.4–3.6 for coal [5].
  • Cheaper to build than coal per kW of capacity [6].
  • Proven at scale: the U.S. leads the world with 3,714 MW installed (2020) [9], and California's The Geysers is the world's largest developed geothermal field [10].

Limitations

  • Plants must be built near usable underground heat, which limits where they can go.
  • Siting needs more geological survey work up front than a fossil-fuel plant.
  • Releases small amounts of hydrogen sulfide, methane and ammonia [8].
  • A site's heat can decline with heavy use, ending its usefulness.
